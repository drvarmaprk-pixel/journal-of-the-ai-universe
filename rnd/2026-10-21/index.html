Executive summary

This edition was dominated by hardware–software co-design for accelerator portability, coinciding with PyTorch Conference North America:

  • AWS released TorchNeuron, an open-source public beta that makes Trainium a native PyTorch device.
  • IBM Research presented Torch-Spyre, built on the same PrivateUse1 extension route.
  • AMD and Intel agreed to unify x86 CPU optimisation for PyTorch in oneDNN and to co-develop AI Compute Extensions (ACE), a matrix-multiplication ISA extension.
  • The llm-d 1.0 open inference platform reached production status with agent-aware scheduling.

In science, a Google DeepMind preprint reported Lean-verified accelerations for 11 previously unanalysed BB(6) Busy Beaver machines. Other developments:

  • Microsoft opened BioEmu-2 (biomolecular ensembles).
  • NVIDIA released Cosmos3-H-Dreams, a real-time generative surgical simulator.
  • Meta disclosed detailed Prometheus supercluster reliability data.
  • OpenAI reported large low-resource-language coding gains.
  • Mistral launched Voxtral 3.
  • The Anthropic-blog GRAFT preprint proposed localising reward hacking in an ablatable adapter.

Most claims are company-reported. Only one item is formally verifiable (the Lean proofs), and none had been independently reproduced at the time of writing.

NVIDIA

HardwareCompany page →

HardwareCo-designCompany or official announcementTRL: Simulation-stageTRL: Lab prototype

1. Company and research division. NVIDIA Research / NVIDIA Healthcare & Robotics (Cosmos world-foundation-model team); clinical data from the CMR Surgical Versius platform.

2. Date of publication / announcement. 20 October 2026 (Hugging Face blog, 'Published October 20, 2026'). Related item: NVIDIA newsroom, 19 October 2026: record 104-qubit fault-tolerant quantum-computing simulation on JUPITER (Jülich); exact release time not confirmed, treated as context.

3. Research title and field. Cosmos3-H-Dreams: Bringing Clinical Surgery to Life Through Generative Simulation. Field: world foundation models, action-conditioned video generation, surgical robotics simulation.

4. Research background. Surgical-robotics training and policy learning rely on numerical simulators that model geometry, contact and tissue mechanics explicitly. These are hard to make photorealistic for in-body scenes, where smoke, specular reflections and occlusion are common. The earlier Cosmos-H-Dreams, built on Cosmos-Predict2.5, learned visual dynamics for dVRK tabletop suturing. This release moves to clinical cholecystectomy.

5. Scientific innovation. Interactive generative simulation of clinical laparoscopic surgery. The model learns visual dynamics directly from synchronised clinical video and robot kinematics, conditioned on live instrument actions. NVIDIA states that, to its knowledge, this is the first real-time generative surgical simulator trained on clinical cholecystectomy recordings that supports live kinematic control.

6. Methodology. The base model is Cosmos3-H-Embodied-Nano, a 16-billion-parameter model pre-trained on Open-H-Embodiment. A bidirectional teacher is specialised on curated cholecystectomy video and kinematics with two engaged instruments (a left Cadiere grasper and a right monopolar hook). The action space has 28 dimensions: pose and gripper for both arms, energy controls, thumbstick axes and buttons. Training uses 832×480 video with actions aligned at 10 Hz. The teacher horizon is extended from 24 to 72 frames (2.4 s to 7.2 s). It is distilled into a causal student through causal teacher forcing and then Self-Forcing, using a learned critic. There are two checkpoints: a 4-step student and a separately trained 2-step student. Streaming inference uses FlashDreams with an adapted FadeMem module that consolidates the KV cache. Evaluation is qualitative: rollouts follow VR-headset-recorded instrument trajectories not seen in training.

7. Results. Runs interactively on a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPU. No quantitative metrics were published: no FVD/PSNR, no action-following error, no latency in milliseconds and no surgeon ratings. The trade-off between 2-step and 4-step generation (speed against visual quality) is described only qualitatively.

8. Scientific significance. This is a credible step towards using learned world models as interactive surgical simulators. It shows that distillation plus streaming-cache management can bring a 16B-parameter world model to real-time control on one workstation GPU. That is a hardware–software co-design result: model distillation is matched to a single-GPU inference budget.

9. Technical limitations. It is a research simulator, explicitly 'not a validated system for patient care'. Visual realism is not biomechanical fidelity. It covers only one procedure (cholecystectomy) and two instruments, with restricted camera commands. Errors accumulate over long rollouts. Without quantitative benchmarks the claims cannot be compared or reproduced.

10. Technology readiness and evidence type. Lab prototype (open research release of code and checkpoints). Evidence type: preliminary demonstration (company technical blog). Not peer-reviewed and not independently reproduced.

11. Competitive comparison. Comparable evidence is scarce. Earlier surgical world models (including NVIDIA's own Cosmos-H-Dreams on dVRK) addressed tabletop tasks. Classical simulators such as SOFA-based platforms model mechanics explicitly but with lower visual realism. No head-to-head benchmark is available.

12. Future research (1–5 years) — FORECAST. FORECAST (1–5 years): hybrid generative–numerical surgical simulators that constrain deformation with explicit mechanics; surgeon-rated benchmarks; closed-loop training of autonomous sub-task policies (e.g. retraction) inside world models; extension to more procedures and instruments; regulatory interest in simulation-based validation evidence.

Alphabet (Google / Google DeepMind)

SoftwareCompany page →

HardwareSoftwarePeer-reviewedPreprintCompany or official announcementTRL: Lab prototypeTRL: Commercial

1. Company and research division. Google DeepMind (with University of Cambridge affiliation for the lead author); secondary item: Google Research Coral NPU (edge hardware IP) in Synaptics Astra SR200.

2. Date of publication / announcement. arXiv:2610.22603. v1 submitted 15 October 2026 and announced 19 October; v2 dated 20 October 2026. The submission dates come from an arXiv mirror/listing; the abstract page itself showed no date. Coral NPU/SR200: Synaptics press release, 20 October 2026.

3. Research title and field. 'At the Limits of Computability: Steps Towards Autonomous AI Discovery on the Busy Beaver Frontier'. Field: AI for mathematics, automated conjecturing, formal verification (Lean 4).

4. Research background. BB(n) is the maximum number of steps a halting n-state, 2-symbol Turing machine can run from a blank tape. It is uncomputable and grows faster than any computable function. The community determined BB(5) = 47,176,870 without AI (STOC 2026). BB(6) still has about 1,094 'holdout' machines whose halting status is unknown. Some of them, called Cryptids, reduce to open Collatz-like problems.

5. Scientific innovation. An autonomous pipeline that discovers structure rather than solving a human-posed problem. It treats Turing machines as grey boxes, conjectures compact macro-transition rules from simulation data, and proves the rules in Lean 4 using a multi-persona agent (Problem Setter, Planner, Writer, Critic). The agent may revise the formal statements it was seeded with, but not the machine definition or the start configuration.

6. Methodology. Every stage uses Gemini 3.1 Pro; a second stage used an auto-research system with Gemini 3.8 Flash. The process runs as follows:

  • Pattern detection: each machine is simulated for S = 5×10^6 steps; tapes are compressed into run-length blocks with a simplicity score, and the top K = 100 patterns are kept.
  • Conjecturing: Python transition functions are written and refined over up to N = 5 turns, using an interactive simulator capped at 128,000 steps per synthetic transition.
  • Filtering: only conjectures with 100% empirical accuracy pass.
  • Informal proving: a generator–evaluator loop with up to M = 4 iterations scores correctness and format.
  • Formal proving: Lean 4.28.1 with BusyLean, a 72-hour cap, and acceptance only with no sorry and no unauthorised axioms.

The search covered 1,094 BB(6) holdouts, 65 BB(2,5) holdouts and 4 BB(3,3) holdouts. The authors manually validated the final Lean statements.

7. Results. 52 candidate machines were found (50 in BB(6)). Formally verified Collatz-like accelerations were obtained for 11 BB(6) machines with no prior documented analysis; the authors provisionally classify these as 'Cryptids'. Formal proofs or higher-level accelerations were also obtained for 15 previously studied machines (14 BB(6) and 1 BB(2,5)). Lean proofs and code are public (github.com/google-deepmind/cryptids).

8. Scientific significance. This is one of the cleaner demonstrations of AI-driven mathematical discovery with machine-checked guarantees. Results can be checked independently by compiling the Lean code, which reduces reliance on trust in the model.

9. Technical limitations. The work is a preprint and has not been peer-reviewed. Cryptid status is 'subject to community acceptance', and the paper's definition is broader than the community's. The approach targets only Collatz-like dynamics and does not resolve halting. Some rediscoveries were proved but not checked by hand. Compute cost is not reported.

10. Technology readiness and evidence type. Method: lab prototype. Evidence: preprint, with formally verified (machine-checked) outputs. Not yet independently reproduced by the bbchallenge community.

11. Competitive comparison. Comparable work: AlphaEvolve (search over code with a reward signal), LEAP and AI Co-Mathematician (assistive), and community use of Claude Opus 4.6 for FRACTRAN BB_f non-halting proofs (cited by the authors). This work is distinctive for combining autonomous conjecturing with end-to-end Lean verification.

12. Future research (1–5 years) — FORECAST. FORECAST (1–5 years): extension beyond Collatz-like dynamics; community adjudication of the 11 machines; similar 'observe–conjecture–formalise' pipelines applied to combinatorics and dynamical systems; Lean-verified AI contributions becoming routine in open-problem catalogues.

1. Company and research division. Microsoft Research AI for Science (BioEmu team led by Frank Noé); secondary item: Microsoft Security (Guardian Agents).

2. Date of publication / announcement. BioEmu-2 public preview announced 20 October 2026, according to a secondary aggregator citing the team announcement. The model card says 'Release date: October 2026'. Guardian Agents: Microsoft Security blog, 19 October 2026 (context).

3. Research title and field. BioEmu-2: a generative foundation model for biomolecular structure and dynamics. Field: AI for science, protein and biomolecular ensembles.

4. Research background. Static structure predictors (AlphaFold class) give one conformation. Function often depends on conformational ensembles, which are expensive to sample with molecular dynamics. BioEmu-1 (Science, 2025) emulated equilibrium ensembles of single protein chains, generating thousands of structures per hour on a single GPU.

5. Scientific innovation. All-atom generative ensembles extended to multi-chain complexes, ligands and nucleic acids, with structure prediction, equilibrium sampling and forward-in-time propagation.

6. Methodology. According to the model card: a diffusion-based all-atom score model of about 500M parameters. Inputs are sequence(s), an optional MSA, and contact and method conditioning. Output is an ensemble sampled by reverse diffusion. Training took place in August–September 2026; licence MIT. It requires Linux and an NVIDIA GPU, and context-parallel sampling needs 4 or more GPUs. Training data and benchmarks for v2 were not described in the sources reviewed.

7. Results. No v2 quantitative results were identified in primary sources at the time of writing. The baseline from BioEmu-1 (peer-reviewed, Science, July 2025) is relative free energies with about 1 kcal/mol accuracy versus millisecond-scale MD and experimental data. That figure applies to v1 only.

8. Scientific significance. If v2's accuracy holds for complexes and ligands, this moves ensemble emulation from single-chain proteins towards drug–target and protein–nucleic-acid systems, which matters for binding-site dynamics and cryptic pockets.

9. Technical limitations. Accuracy claims for v2 are not yet peer-reviewed. Generated ensembles are plausible samples, not physically guaranteed trajectories. Performance outside the training distribution (e.g. membrane proteins, large assemblies) is unknown. The hardware requirement ties it to NVIDIA GPUs.

10. Technology readiness and evidence type. Lab prototype / research public preview with open weights. Evidence type: preliminary (model card plus team announcement); v1 is peer-reviewed.

11. Competitive comparison. Comparable systems include AlphaFold-class static predictors and MD-based ensembles. No peer-reviewed v2-versus-competitor benchmark was found.

12. Future research (1–5 years) — FORECAST. FORECAST (1–5 years): peer-reviewed validation of v2 against MD and experimental ensembles (NMR, HDX); coupling with docking and free-energy pipelines in drug discovery; use for mechanism hypotheses in cardiovascular and oncology targets.

Amazon (AWS)

HardwareCompany page →

HardwareCo-designCompany or official announcementTRL: Pilot deployment

1. Company and research division. AWS Annapurna Labs / AWS Neuron team, with the PyTorch Foundation and Meta PyTorch team (extension points).

2. Date of publication / announcement. 20 October 2026: PyTorch blog last updated 2026-10-20. The open-source public beta went live on 20 October at PyTorch Conference North America.

3. Research title and field. TorchNeuron: Native PyTorch backend for AWS Trainium. Field: accelerator software stacks, compilers, hardware–software co-design.

4. Research background. Custom AI accelerators often fail to gain adoption because model code, libraries and tooling assume CUDA. PyTorch has added accelerator-agnostic extension points (PrivateUse1, torch.accelerator) so that out-of-tree hardware can appear as a first-class device.

5. Scientific innovation. Trainium is registered as a native 'neuron' PyTorch device. It supports eager mode, a new experimental adaptive eager mode, torch.compile (TorchDynamo → FX → AOTAutograd → Torch-MLIR → StableHLO → Neuron compiler → NEFF), NKI custom kernels, distributed APIs and native streams and events. Profiling is integrated with torch.profiler and Neuron Explorer, with Parquet output that agents can consume.

6. Methodology. Testing used:

  • a 'blind sweep' of about 100k Hugging Face models (said to cover 99.9% of real-world usage by downloads) on reduced configurations, checking load, forward/backward, compile and CPU fallback, as of September 2026
  • the PyTorch upstream test suites (ATen, Dynamo, distributed, profiler), run in August 2026

The example workload was Llama 3 8B trained with TorchTitan on 8×trn2.48xlarge (TP + HSDP). Hardware: Trainium2 and Trainium3. Data types: BF16, FP32, FP16; FP8 and TorchAO are in private beta.

7. Results. Upstream tests produced about 154K outcomes, of which about 69K were skipped: about 30K by PyTorch's own eligibility checks and about 39K deliberate Neuron skips, including about 22K for operators without a native lowering. Per-model sweep pass rates were shown only graphically. Customer claim: Deepgram reports about 60% more input audio seconds processed per dollar than its GPU baselines on its own benchmarks. The vendor's 'Getting started' code requires a one-line device change.

8. Scientific significance. This is a significant portability milestone. It reduces the switching cost that has protected the CUDA ecosystem and gives Hugging Face Transformers v5, Diffusers and TRL a native non-NVIDIA training and inference path.

9. Technical limitations. The release is a public beta. Not supported: dynamic or symbolic shapes, sparse tensors, NestedTensor, forward-mode AD, arbitrary attention masks and some upsampling modes. Non-default streams multiplex onto a single hardware queue. Performance parity with hand-tuned NeuronX stacks is not quantified.

10. Technology readiness and evidence type. Pilot deployment (open-source public beta on commercial hardware, with named customers). Evidence type: vendor technical blog; customer figures are marketing claims; not independently reproduced.

11. Competitive comparison. It follows the same pattern as IBM Torch-Spyre (PrivateUse1 + Inductor) and other out-of-tree backends. Google TPU uses PyTorch/XLA and AMD uses ROCm in-tree. No comparable cross-vendor benchmark was published.

12. Future research (1–5 years) — FORECAST. FORECAST (1–5 years): device-agnostic PyTorch becoming the default for training custom silicon; FP8 general availability in the next release; closure of the operator gaps; faster adoption of Trainium for open-model fine-tuning; pressure on other accelerator vendors to ship PrivateUse1 backends.

SoftwareCo-designPreprintCompany or official announcementTRL: Lab prototypeTRL: Commercial

1. Company and research division. Meta Infrastructure / Engineering at Meta, with Meta Superintelligence Labs and NVIDIA (co-engineering); separately, Meta AI research (RPL paper).

2. Date of publication / announcement. Prometheus: Engineering at Meta, 19 October 2026 (exact publication time not confirmed). RPL: AI at Meta publication page dated 20 October 2026.

3. Research title and field. (a) 'Inside Prometheus: Meta's Journey to a Gigawatt-Scale Supercluster'. Field: AI training infrastructure, reliability. (b) 'Learning to Program Retrievals: Composing Search Primitives via Reinforcement Learning' (RPL). Field: retrieval-augmented agents, RL.

4. Research background. At frontier scale, a single fault can halt synchronous training. Goodput and mean time between interruptions (MTBI) therefore become first-order determinants of effective compute.

5. Scientific innovation. (a) Disclosure of fleet-scale reliability engineering for NVL72 domains: in-rack sparing, parallelism-aware fault tolerance, firmware automation and power-transient management. (b) RPL lets a policy compose search primitives into executable programs with local state. 'Program mutation' is a grammar-constrained, execution-verified way to generate improved trajectories that are distilled back into the policy.

6. Methodology. (a) Hardware platforms: Catalina (GB200 NVL72 'Ariel', 800G scale-out per GPU), Clemente (GB300 NVL72) and the forthcoming San Miguel (Vera Rubin NVL72). Networking: building fabrics of up to 20,736 GPUs (DSF, then NSF on 51.2T switches) and BAG aggregation over 2 km to more than 100 km (Arista 7800 / Broadcom Jericho3). Training stack: PyTorch with FSDP, plus Tectonic and LogDevice storage. (b) Evaluation on open-domain QA and corpus-level reasoning at two model sizes (4B and 8B).

7. Results. (a) Goodput above 85% for pre-training and above 90% for RL (Muse Wonder). MTBI was about 47k GPU-hours during those runs and is now above 54k GPU-hours. Interruptions fell by 99% from a mid-2025 peak of about 1,000 events per 8K GPUs per day; the target is 2 events per 8K GPUs per day (about 100k GPU-hours MTBI). Sparing: a 64-of-72 slice plus about 2% spare racks means 523 racks (37,656 GPUs) for a 32,768-GPU job, about 13% overhead, of which 11% comes from slice geometry. Firmware P90 per-pod upgrade time fell from more than 7 h to 2 h. Power transients swing 30–40%. The San Miguel spec claims 2.52 EFLOPS FP4 per rack and a 10–20% performance-per-watt gain over GB300. (b) A 4B RPL model outperformed all 8B baselines on open-domain QA, according to the abstract; it also had the highest answer F1 and document-coverage F1 on corpus-level tasks, with fewer retrieval turns.

8. Scientific significance. This is rare quantitative disclosure of how reliability, rather than peak FLOPS, bounds frontier training, and it is highly relevant to co-design of scale-up domains. RPL shows that small models can learn retrieval strategies that beat larger fixed pipelines.

9. Technical limitations. (a) Company-reported figures that have not been audited. The primary page returned HTTP 403 to our fetch, so the text was verified via a verbatim mirror. (b) RPL is an arXiv preprint; the full paper was not reviewed beyond the abstract.

10. Technology readiness and evidence type. (a) Commercial (operational infrastructure); evidence: company engineering disclosure. (b) Lab prototype; evidence: preprint.

11. Competitive comparison. Comparable published figures are few. Earlier Meta 24K and 100K-GPU cluster reports, and Llama 3 training-interruption statistics (2024), provide baselines. No other hyperscaler disclosed comparable MTBI data at the time of writing.

12. Future research (1–5 years) — FORECAST. FORECAST (1–5 years): reliability (MTBI) becomes a procurement metric alongside FLOPS; larger scale-up domains drive in-rack sparing as standard; Vera Rubin integration in 2027; RPL-style programmable retrieval inside agent frameworks.

1. Company and research division. OpenAI (research and applied teams).

2. Date of publication / announcement. 'Advancing coding in low-resource programming languages', 19 October 2026 (widely discussed on 20 October). ChatGPT release notes, 20 October 2026: GPT-6 Pro parallel agents; GPT-Live-1.1 voices.

3. Research title and field. Low-resource programming-language capability (COBOL, Fortran, OCaml, Visual Basic) and two new evaluations: MultiLangBench and a COBOL migration evaluation. Field: code generation, evaluation.

4. Research background. Legacy languages have little public training data but run critical infrastructure. OpenAI cites COBOL's role in more than 40% of online banking systems and 95% of ATM transactions.

5. Scientific innovation. Long-horizon evaluation of building a SQLite-compatible engine from scratch in 20 languages, using database files as a language-neutral interface, together with a behaviour-preserving migration test requiring byte-for-byte parity on hidden tests.

6. Methodology. MultiLangBench adapts tests from SQLite's own suite. The candidate program receives a database and a SQL query and must output the modified database, which is compared against reference SQLite. It is reported as test pass rate and API cost. The COBOL migration evaluation targets C++, Java, Kotlin, Python and Rust; success requires all hidden tests to pass.

7. Results. At maximum effort, GPT-6 Astra averaged 87.4% (versus 26.8% for GPT-5.6 Sol). By language: COBOL 10.9%→72.1%, OCaml 25.6%→85.8%, Fortran 21.4%→83.5%, Visual Basic 27.5%→89.4%; Astra scores above 83% in 19 of 20 languages. GPT-6 Luna reached 40.3% at an estimated $0.77 per sample versus $3.48 (about 78% lower). COBOL migration: GPT-6.1 Sol scored 86.6% at low effort (versus 5.4%, at 61% of the cost) and 97.9% at higher effort.

8. Scientific significance. Strong evidence that frontier models now generalise to low-resource languages on long-horizon tasks. The open-sourced test suite allows external replication.

9. Technical limitations. These are company-run evaluations of its own models. The migration evaluation is near saturation. Real legacy systems involve JCL, CICS, copybooks and undocumented business rules not captured here. OpenAI itself notes that expert review is still required.

10. Technology readiness and evidence type. Commercial (models available via API). Evidence type: company evaluation (marketing claim, with the benchmark released for reproduction).

11. Competitive comparison. No third-party MultiLangBench results for competitors were available at the time of writing. Other vendors' COBOL tools (for example IBM watsonx Code Assistant for Z) report different metrics, so the figures are not directly comparable.

12. Future research (1–5 years) — FORECAST. FORECAST (1–5 years): independent leaderboards on MultiLangBench; AI-assisted mainframe migration programmes in banking and government; regulators asking for behaviour-equivalence evidence.

Hardware–software co-design analysis

1. The software moat is being re-engineered at the framework layer. TorchNeuron (AWS) and Torch-Spyre (IBM) both register non-CUDA silicon through PyTorch's PrivateUse1 extension point and lower torch.compile graphs through vendor compilers. TorchNeuron uses Torch-MLIR, StableHLO and NEFF; Torch-Spyre uses Inductor with scratchpad-aware tiling. The common lesson, explicit in both disclosures, is that dataflow and scratchpad accelerators need device-specific tensor layouts and tiling hidden behind standard PyTorch semantics. IBM's companion talks (MAKCI analytical cost models; model-derived operator tests covering 116 operators from 8 models) and AWS's report of about 22K skipped upstream tests show where the remaining cost lies. That cost is operator coverage, dynamic shapes and correctness on real-model shapes, not peak FLOPS.

2. CPU-side convergence. The AMD–Intel oneDNN/ACE agreement extends the same logic down to the ISA. One library becomes the PyTorch default for x86, and a jointly designed matrix-multiply extension is planned. If realised, compilers target one x86 matrix ISA instead of vendor-specific variants. This is hardware designed around a shared software abstraction, which is co-design in the strict sense.

3. Scale-up domains trade bandwidth for reliability. Meta's Prometheus data quantify a little-discussed cost. Moving from NVL8 to NVL72 enlarges the fault blast radius by 9×, and the response is architectural: 64-of-72 slices, in-rack spares, about 13% provisioning overhead and parallelism-aware fault tolerance. The model-parallel layout (power-of-two groups) therefore directly shapes hardware utilisation. NVLink signal integrity (more than 600K links at 200 Gb/s per lane) and HBM/LPDDR errors are the dominant failure modes. These are physical-layer constraints that software must absorb.

4. Inference economics favour memory-centric serving. llm-d 1.0's agent-aware routing, prefix-cache hit rates (about 98% in the IBM case) and prefill/decode disaggregation reflect a shift in which context management, not generation, dominates cost. This raises demand for KV-cache capacity and fast cache tiers. It is consistent with the memory-capacity-heavy inference designs noted earlier in October, such as Intel's D-Series with 480 GB LPDDR5X.

5. Algorithms constrained to the edge and to single GPUs. NVIDIA distilled a 16B-parameter surgical world model to 2–4 denoising steps for interactive use on one RTX PRO 6000. Mistral overlaps ASR, LLM and TTS stages to keep voice-agent turns under 1 s. Google Research's Coral NPU (open design) is now inside Synaptics SR200 MCUs at 400 GOPS. In each case the model or pipeline was reshaped to fit a fixed hardware budget.

6. Verification as infrastructure. DeepMind's Busy Beaver pipeline relies on Lean 4 as a 'hardware-independent' trust layer, and GRAFT uses monitors to route gradients rather than rewards. Both are methodological answers to the same co-design question: how to get reliable outputs from imperfect generators.

Emerging research bottlenecks

  1. Operator coverage and dynamic shapes for non-CUDA accelerators. AWS reports about 22K upstream tests skipped for operators without a native lowering, and does not yet support dynamic or symbolic shapes. These are still the main friction for MoE routing and agent workloads on custom silicon.
  2. Reliability at scale-up-domain granularity. NVL72 blast radius, NVLink signal integrity and HBM/LPDDR error rates directly cap goodput (Meta).
  3. Memory supply and base-die logistics. Recent press reports describe HBM4E base dies moving to foundry logic nodes, and Tesla has cut LPDDR on AI5/AI6 because of DRAM shortages. The press reports are unconfirmed, but they are consistent with a memory-constrained 2026–2027.
  4. Leading-edge foundry capacity. Secondary coverage reports TSMC 3 nm at 33% of 3Q26 wafer revenue, with lines running full. It is a binding constraint for accelerator start-ups.
  5. Power transients. Synchronous training swings of 30–40% and multi-ten-megawatt steps need cluster-level capping and storage (Meta Dynamo Dimmer, BBU/CBU/BESS).
  6. Evaluation credibility. Most of the day's quantitative claims were self-reported, LLM-judged or chart-only. Independent, reproducible benchmarks are lacking, notably for speech, coding and world models.
  7. Clinical validation of generative medical models. Cosmos3-H-Dreams and BioEmu-2 both lack published quantitative validation against expert ratings or experimental ensembles.
  8. Oversight with weak monitors. Reward hacking that evades monitors remains open. GRAFT is promising but untested with LLM judges or in multi-turn RL.
